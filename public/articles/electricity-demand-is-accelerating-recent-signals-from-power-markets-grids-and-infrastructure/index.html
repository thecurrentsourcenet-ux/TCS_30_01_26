Electricity Demand Is Accelerating: Recent Signals from Power Markets, Grids, and Infrastructure

1. Power Demand Is Entering a New Growth Phase

Recent data from the International Energy Agency's Electricity 2026 report points to a structural acceleration in global electricity demand. Power consumption is projected to grow by an average of 3.5% annually through 2030—more than twice the pace of total energy demand growth.

The scale of this expansion is material. According to the IEA, the increase in global electricity consumption expected by 2030 is equivalent to adding more than two European Unions to the global power system. This shift reflects converging drivers: industrial electrification, electric vehicle adoption, rising cooling demand, and the rapid expansion of data centres and artificial intelligence workloads.

Notably, electricity demand in advanced economies is rising again for the first time in 15 years, accounting for roughly one-fifth of global growth. Meeting this demand trajectory will require a substantial response: the IEA estimates that annual grid investment must increase by around 50%, alongside a decisive shift toward system flexibility to manage weather-dependent generation.

2. Generation Mix: Renewables and Nuclear Gain Ground

The global power mix continues to decarbonise. Renewables and nuclear energy are on track to supply around 50% of global electricity generation by 2030, up from roughly 42% today.

A key milestone was reached in 2025, when renewables—driven primarily by record solar PV deployment—effectively matched coal-fired generation. By the end of the decade, coal is expected to play a secondary role in the global power system.

Nuclear output is also rebounding. EU nuclear generation increased by 4.8% in 2024, with particularly high shares in countries such as France (67.3% of generation) and Slovakia (61.6%).

At the same time, natural gas remains a critical part of the system. Gas-fired generation continues to grow, supported by rising demand in the United States and fuel-switching strategies in parts of the Middle East. Despite decarbonisation objectives, gas is increasingly viewed as a stabilising bridge fuel as coal capacity retires and system security concerns persist.

3. Grid Reliability Is Under Growing Pressure

Grid constraints are emerging as a defining challenge of the current power transition. The New York Independent System Operator's 2025–2034 Comprehensive Reliability Plan provides a concrete illustration of these pressures.

To maintain system stability, NYISO increasingly relies on Emergency Operating Procedures, ranging from public conservation appeals to voltage reductions. The plan projects that such interventions could triple in frequency by 2034.

Demand Response was activated 22 times in 2025 alone. While effective in the short term, repeated reliance on large consumers to curtail load raises the risk of "response fatigue," potentially undermining future participation.

NYISO identifies three forces tightening reserve margins:

Capacity imbalance: Retirements of ageing fossil-fuel plants are outpacing additions of firm capacity.

Electrification load: Data centres, heat pumps, and other electrified end uses are pushing peak demand higher.

Winter fuel risk: Natural gas constraints during cold weather threaten availability precisely when heating demand peaks.

In response, system planners are moving beyond single-path forecasts toward multi-future planning and stress-testing, explicitly modelling delayed transmission projects and extreme weather scenarios.

4. Infrastructure Investment and Long-Term Growth

Long-term evidence reinforces the economic relevance of these infrastructure challenges. A 30-year study covering 92 countries finds that a sustained 5% increase in infrastructure stock is associated with a 0.45 percentage-point increase in long-run GDP growth.

Returns vary significantly by development stage:

In developed economies, investment in energy generation and high-quality transport infrastructure delivers the strongest gains, with motorways yielding higher returns than basic road expansion.

In developing economies, rail infrastructure shows particularly strong growth elasticity.

In the least developed countries, infrastructure investment depends critically on complementary human capital, without which returns are materially lower.

These findings are particularly relevant as governments scale up grid and energy investment under tight fiscal conditions and delivery constraints.

5. The Delivery Challenge: Protecting Infrastructure Value

Across Western economies, infrastructure delivery performance remains a major risk. On average, large projects experience cost overruns of around 55% and schedule delays of 35%.

In Europe alone, estimated infrastructure modernisation needs reach €12 trillion by 2040, including €3.6 trillion for building retrofits. Protecting value at this scale requires changes across the delivery chain:

Ecosystem level: Streamlined permitting processes, such as "single front door" systems. The Netherlands' Spatial Planning Act offers a reference point.

Portfolio level: Lean, expert-led delivery organisations, exemplified by the Société du Grand Paris.

Project level: Procurement models aligned with complexity, supported by reference class forecasting and early risk investigation.

Digital tools are also gaining relevance. Singapore's "Virtual Singapore" digital twin, integrating thousands of sensors, has already demonstrated its ability to reduce flood damage and improve mobility planning outcomes.

6. Hydrogen: Scaling Beyond Pilot Phase

Hydrogen remains a central option for decarbonising hard-to-electrify sectors. Japan's Fukushima Hydrogen Energy Research Field illustrates how hydrogen projects are being positioned as both energy and regional development initiatives. To support scale-up, the Japanese government has committed approximately ¥3 trillion in subsidies over 15 years.

The sector remains divided between:

Green hydrogen, produced via renewable-powered electrolysis, including off-grid pilots such as ACCIONA's project in Chile.

Blue hydrogen, which relies on fossil fuels with carbon capture and faces ongoing scrutiny over lifecycle emissions and asset-stranding risks.

In Europe, cross-border hydrogen infrastructure is advancing, supported by €650 million in EU funding across 14 projects. Germany is emerging as a leader in hydrogen storage and terminals, while Spain leads on electricity interconnectivity.

7. Energy Security and the Return of Fossil Fuel Investment

Since 2022, global energy policy has shifted toward a more explicit security focus. Natural gas is increasingly treated as a strategic transition fuel rather than solely a decarbonisation liability.

Italy has eased exploration restrictions, and Greece has awarded its first offshore gas exploration licence in four decades. The Ionian Sea Block 2 alone is estimated to hold up to 200 billion cubic metres of gas.

In an environment of geopolitical uncertainty, domestic production is increasingly framed as compatible with short- to medium-term energy security objectives, even as long-term climate targets remain in place.

8. Outlook to 2030: Planning for Uncertainty

The accelerating electrification of economies is reshaping power systems faster than infrastructure delivery models have historically accommodated. National resilience in this environment depends on stress-tested planning, flexible grids, and active public-sector management.

With constrained resources and increasingly complex supply chains, the infrastructure challenge is no longer just about scale. Selecting the right projects—and delivering them efficiently—has become a prerequisite for economic stability and energy security through 2030 and beyond.
