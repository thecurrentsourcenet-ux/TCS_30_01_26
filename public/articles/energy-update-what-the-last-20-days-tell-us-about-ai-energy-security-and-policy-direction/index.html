Energy Update: What the Last 20 Days Tell Us About AI, Energy Security and Policy Direction

Over the last 20 days, two International Energy Agency themes have become especially relevant for energy professionals: the rapid expansion of the AI-data centre-energy nexus, and the broader reordering of energy policy in response to security risks, affordability pressures and supply-chain constraints.

Taken together, these developments do not point to a single "big trend". They show a system that is being reshaped by three forces at the same time: rising electricity demand from digital infrastructure, renewed concern over energy security, and a more pragmatic policy environment in which competitiveness and cost are increasingly central.

Ad Space

**AI is becoming a material electricity and infrastructure issue**

The latest IEA work shows that electricity demand from data centres grew by 17% in 2025, while electricity consumption from AI-focused data centres grew by 50% in the same year. The central projection remains that total data-centre electricity demand rises from 485 TWh in 2025 to around 950 TWh in 2030, or about 3% of global electricity demand by that date.

This matters because the discussion has moved beyond whether AI consumes energy. The more relevant issue now is where the pressure appears first and how it is transmitted through the system.

The first pressure point is physical infrastructure. The report highlights tightening bottlenecks in grid connections, power equipment, advanced chips and capital. Planning systems are being stretched by the volume of project applications, while shortages in areas such as high-bandwidth memory and electrical equipment are becoming more visible.

In practical terms, this means that data-centre growth is no longer only a question of digital demand; it is also a question of transformers, power electronics, interconnection queues and local acceptance.

The second pressure point is load profile. AI-related computing is not simply adding demand; it is changing how demand behaves. The IEA notes that AI server power density rose eleven-fold between 2020 and 2025 and could rise another fourfold by 2027. AI data centres also create large and rapid load swings, which makes storage increasingly important. This is why batteries are moving from being an optional resilience feature to a core operational component of some advanced facilities.

The third point is that efficiency alone does not resolve the issue. Per-query energy use has fallen sharply, especially for simple text tasks, but newer applications such as reasoning, video generation and agentic workflows are far more energy-intensive. For readers, the important conclusion is that improvements in model efficiency need to be assessed alongside growth in usage and changes in the type of workloads being deployed.

**The supply response is becoming more diverse, but also more complicated**

One of the clearest messages in the source material is that data-centre developers are not relying on one solution. Grid connection remains the preferred route in most cases, but slow interconnection processes are pushing some developers toward on-site generation, storage and firm-power procurement.

The IEA points to a growing pipeline of on-site natural-gas projects for US data centres, while also noting major interest in batteries, demand response, existing nuclear assets and small modular reactors. This does not mean one technology has "won". It means that the market is responding to connection constraints and reliability requirements by assembling a broader mix of options.

For energy-market participants, this has two implications. First, AI-linked demand is beginning to create targeted upside for specific parts of the power sector, especially gas turbines, electrical equipment, storage and some nuclear-related activity. Second, this demand is not large enough to lift the entire energy sector equally. The report is clear that financial markets are not pricing AI as a broad-based boost for all energy companies.

That distinction is important for readers evaluating where value is likely to accrue. The most immediate effects are concentrated in enabling infrastructure, not across the whole sector.

**Energy security has returned to the centre of policy**

The second major theme is energy security. The IEA's State of Energy Policy 2026 summary shows that governments are operating in a sustained period of disruption shaped by pandemic after-effects, the war in Ukraine, trade restrictions, extreme weather and conflicts affecting major suppliers.

The immediate trigger in the latest period was the conflict in the Middle East and the associated disruption to energy markets. The collective release of 400 million barrels from IEA emergency reserves on 11 March 2026 is an example of how post-1970s emergency frameworks are now being used in practice. More countries have stockholding rules, more have gas-storage requirements, and more have emergency response mechanisms than in previous decades.

For readers, the significance is not only that these tools exist. It is that governments are using them while also reassessing longer-term exposure to imports, concentrated supply chains and price volatility. The policy response therefore has both a short-term and a structural dimension.

In the short term, countries are deploying affordability measures, storage releases, conservation campaigns and, in some cases, fuel-rationing or demand-restraint measures. In the longer term, they are strengthening renewables, nuclear, efficiency, electrification and supply-chain diversification policies.

This is one of the most useful contextual points for readers: current energy policy cannot be read only through a climate lens or only through a security lens. It is being shaped by both, along with fiscal and industrial constraints.

**The policy environment is becoming more pragmatic and more uneven**

The sources also show that policy direction in 2025 and early 2026 is not uniformly tightening in favour of decarbonisation. The IEA notes that cost of living, industrial competitiveness and resilient supply chains have become central themes alongside security, efficiency and sustainability.

This helps explain why policy signals can appear contradictory. Governments continue to support clean-energy manufacturing, electrification and access, but some are also delaying standards, reducing some subsidies, rolling back selected regulations or giving greater weight to affordability concerns.

A few figures illustrate this shift. Energy-related government support is estimated at around USD 405 billion annually in 2025. At the same time, several previously expected spending provisions have been revised downward. Regulatory rollbacks in 2025 affected more energy demand than new stricter regulations did, especially in areas such as passenger vehicles. Carbon pricing has also faced delays or adjustments in some jurisdictions.

This does not mean the energy transition has stopped. It means the policy framework is becoming more selective and more cost-conscious. That is a more useful interpretation for readers than describing the moment as either acceleration or reversal.

**Supply chains remain a strategic vulnerability**

A further point that deserves attention is supply-chain concentration. The IEA material highlights that eleven of the twenty critical minerals essential to the energy sector were subject to export controls at some point in 2025, and that 45 new policies affecting trade in key clean-energy technologies were implemented in addition to the broad tariff measures introduced by the United States.

This matters for both digital infrastructure and the energy transition more broadly. AI-related demand intensifies pressure not only on electricity systems, but also on batteries, advanced electronics, cooling systems and grid equipment. At the same time, the broader clean-energy economy still depends heavily on concentrated manufacturing bases for solar, batteries and related components.

For readers, the practical takeaway is that resilience is no longer just a procurement issue. It is becoming a strategic planning issue affecting project timelines, capex, permitting assumptions and risk management.

**What readers should take from this**

There are three grounded conclusions from the source material.

First, the AI-energy connection is no longer marginal. It is now relevant to electricity demand, grid planning, storage, equipment supply chains and local permitting. Readers should treat AI not as a technology-side story, but as a power-system and infrastructure story as well.

Second, energy security is not a temporary overlay. It is shaping policy design, market interventions and investment priorities in ways that interact directly with transition goals. Understanding current policy requires following emergency measures, affordability concerns and industrial strategy alongside decarbonisation frameworks.

Third, the main issue is not a lack of technologies. The sources show that governments and markets already have multiple tools available: renewables, storage, efficiency, electrification, nuclear, flexible tariffs, demand response, strategic reserves and manufacturing incentives. The challenge is coordination across timelines that move at different speeds. Digital demand can scale quickly; grids, permitting and public acceptance do not.
