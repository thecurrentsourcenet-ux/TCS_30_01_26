Global energy markets in March 2026 are characterised by extremes: record‑breaking electricity demand driven by artificial‑intelligence data centres, a deepening energy crisis sparked by conflict in the Middle East, and a flurry of renewable‑energy milestones that demonstrate how quickly the energy transition is progressing. This report draws on public‑domain sources to provide a concise digest of recent events and emerging trends in the style of The CurrentSource.

**Surging electricity demand and AI‑powered data centres**

Ad Space

The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) expects electricity consumption in the United States to keep climbing after two consecutive record highs. Its Short‑Term Energy Outlook, published on 10 March 2026, projects that U.S. power demand will rise from 4,195 billion kWh in 2025 to 4,260 billion kWh in 2026 and 4,388 billion kWh in 2027.

The EIA attributes the surge to "data centres dedicated to artificial intelligence and cryptocurrency" as well as households and businesses using more electricity for space heating and transport.

Despite higher loads, the EIA expects the share of electricity generated from renewable resources to grow. Renewables provided roughly 24% of U.S. generation in 2025 and are projected to reach 25% in 2026 and 27% in 2027. Natural‑gas‑fired power is forecast to hold steady at 40% in 2026 before slipping to 39% in 2027, while coal's share falls from 17% in 2025 to 15% in 2027.

The EIA also notes that households, commercial users and industrial facilities will buy less natural gas, even as gas use for power generation ticks higher.

**Geopolitical shocks and energy security**

**Iran war and the third energy crisis of the 2020s**

The war between the U.S./Israel and Iran has produced the third major energy shock of the decade. After Iran closed the vital Strait of Hormuz—a chokepoint through which around 20% of the world's oil and LNG flows—governments worldwide scrambled to reduce their reliance on imported fossil fuels. A Reuters analysis notes that policymakers are examining proposals to expand nuclear and renewable energy, increase strategic stockpiles and diversify supply sources.

In the near‑term, authorities released emergency petroleum reserves and urged consumers to conserve energy. The International Energy Agency (IEA) called the closure the worst disruption to global energy supplies in history, and Brent crude prices surged above $100 per barrel. Analysts warn that continued disruption could push oil prices towards $200 per barrel.

The shock has also revived interest in nuclear power across Asia and Europe: Taiwanese and Japanese politicians are reconsidering reactor restarts, while European leaders have announced financial guarantees for innovative atomic technologies.

**Policy responses and coal rebound**

To ease the supply crunch and curb consumption, the International Energy Agency advised member governments to adopt measures ranging from reducing highway speeds to encouraging working from home. European leaders held emergency summits where Spain's prime minister criticized attempts to dilute climate policies in response to the crisis. Yet the same crisis has prompted some Asian utilities to increase coal‑fired generation to keep costs down.

This divergence underscores the tension between short‑term energy security and long‑term decarbonization.

Oil‑market volatility has also been exacerbated by U.S. domestic politics. Despite calls for restraint, the U.S. sent thousands of troops to the Middle East and threatened to destroy Iran's energy infrastructure if Hormuz remains closed. Traders are increasingly sceptical that diplomatic efforts will end the conflict soon, and the Brent benchmark has risen 53% since late February, while West Texas Intermediate crude is up 45%.

**Renewable‑energy milestones and corporate action**

Despite geopolitical turmoil, clean‑energy developments are accelerating worldwide. Highlights from March 2026 include:

**European advances**

- Spain doubles renewable capacity: Spain has doubled its solar and wind capacity over the last six years, demonstrating how investment in clean energy shields economies from fossil‑fuel price volatility. - Australia's grid passes 50% renewables: Australia's largest electricity network met record demand while renewables supplied over half of its power. Millions of rooftop solar panels and a growing fleet of battery storage delivered most of the extra energy.

- UK geothermal breakthrough: In Cornwall, the United Downs plant became the UK's first deep‑geothermal power plant, providing 24/7 renewable electricity for up to 10,000 homes and simultaneously extracting lithium. Drilling three miles underground allows water heated to nearly 200°C to drive turbines. Octopus Energy will buy the power and sell it via the grid.

- Portuguese utility profits from renewables: EDP, historically a fossil‑fuel utility, reported a 44% increase in profits thanks to rapid expansion of its renewable‑energy business, while operating costs fell due to efficiency improvements. - Texas eclipses California in utility‑scale solar: With roughly 6.5 GW of new solar capacity added in 2025, Texas has overtaken California as the largest U.S. market for utility‑scale solar.

- Heat‑pump incentives in the Netherlands and UK: The Dutch government plans to close the price gap between heat pumps and gas boilers for about 2.3 million social‑housing units. The UK's £6.6 billion Warm Homes Plan will retrofit millions of homes with insulation and low‑carbon heating, cutting bills while accelerating the shift away from fossil fuels.

- ABB's industrial decarbonization: At its Bella Vista manufacturing site in New South Wales, ABB has cut operational emissions by 97% through electrifying manufacturing processes and switching to clean energy. - Corporate clean‑power deals: Nike and Mitsui signed a solar power purchase agreement that will supply 70% of Nike's electricity demand in Japan. French steel producer ArcelorMittal announced a €1.3 billion investment in a green‑steel plant in Dunkirk.

In Finland, the world's largest steam‑producing heat pump is now supplying industrial steam for district heating, replacing fossil‑fuel boilers. - Policies keeping momentum: More than 20 U.S. states with clean‑electricity or net‑zero targets continue to push ahead with climate policies despite federal rollbacks. Ireland has expanded grants for electric trucks and buses, and Australia's EV subsidies helped achieve a 1.9% drop in annual carbon emissions.

The UK has accelerated renewable‑energy auctions and backed around 190 clean‑energy projects. New sustainability‑reporting standards in the UK and forthcoming ISSB‑aligned climate reporting requirements for South Korea by 2028 will compel companies to disclose more consistent climate data.

**North American and global developments**

- Mexico's EV push: International investors have committed $500 million to build electric vehicles and charging infrastructure in Mexico, accelerating the nation's transition to electrified transport. - Electric‑vehicle surge in Asia and Africa: In India, sales of electric cars and trucks nearly doubled in 2025, and EVs are approaching the 5% market‑share tipping point for mainstream adoption.

Ethiopia's restrictions on internal‑combustion‑engine car imports are driving a surge in electric‑vehicle adoption, reducing gasoline subsidies and improving air quality.

These milestones illustrate that, despite geopolitical turbulence, the global energy transition continues to gather pace. Renewable technologies are being deployed at scale, electric vehicles are becoming mainstream, and new sources like geothermal and industrial heat pumps are moving from pilot to commercial stages.

**Energy‑policy trends to watch in 2026**

Legal analysts at ArentFox Schiff LLP identify several themes likely to shape U.S. energy policy in 2026. Key issues include:

**Powering data centres**: AI‑driven data centres are reshaping load forecasts and prompting operators to invest in behind‑the‑meter gas generation, microgrids and small modular reactors for 24/7 power. Policymakers are debating cost allocation and resilience requirements.

**Energy dominance strategy**: The Trump administration's emphasis on maximizing domestic production of oil, gas, geothermal and nuclear power seeks to assert federal authority over energy projects and could override some state climate policies. Watch for executive actions, expanded exports and litigation over federal–state jurisdiction.

**FERC market redesign**: Efforts to reform interconnection queues, capacity markets and transmission planning continue as RTOs and ISOs grapple with reliability and cost concerns. New rules could affect project financing and the co‑location of data centres and renewables.

**Energy storage and distributed generation**: Battery and distributed‑energy projects are becoming central to grid flexibility but face local opposition, safety concerns and supply‑chain risks. Regulatory clarity on interconnection, aggregation and compensation will shape adoption.

**New nuclear projects and SMRs**: Interest in small modular reactors is high, but commercial viability remains unproven. Federal tax incentives, licensing milestones and commitments from data‑centre operators will determine whether early projects move ahead.

**State–federal jurisdiction and permitting reform**: Tensions between state climate goals and federal "energy dominance" policies are leading to litigation over offshore wind and other projects. Bipartisan momentum for permitting reform seeks to shorten approval timelines, though environmental safeguards may be weakened.

**Trade, tariffs and affordability**: Tariffs on solar components, batteries and critical minerals could raise project costs. Rising electricity rates have made affordability a central political issue, and policymakers are considering targeted relief for vulnerable customers.

**Clean Air Act**: Ongoing debates about the scope of the Clean Air Act and attempts to weaken or revise it will affect greenhouse‑gas regulation.

Together, these issues underscore a complex policy landscape in which rapid electrification, geopolitical disruptions and regulatory uncertainty coexist. Decision‑makers must balance reliability, affordability and decarbonization while navigating evolving federal and state roles.

**Outlook**

March 2026 has shown that energy news and policy can change rapidly, with technological breakthroughs and geopolitical crises occurring simultaneously. The world is experiencing record electricity demand alongside unprecedented supply shocks, yet it is also witnessing historic renewable‑energy achievements such as the UK's first geothermal plant and large‑scale adoption of rooftop solar in Australia.

Governments face the dual challenge of maintaining energy security and accelerating the clean‑energy transition, while corporations and consumers increasingly embrace electrification and sustainability.

Staying informed about both the immediate impacts of conflicts and the longer‑term trajectory of clean‑energy innovation is essential for businesses, policymakers and citizens seeking to navigate the energy landscape.
